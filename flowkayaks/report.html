SECTION 01 — METHODOLOGY

My CRO operating system

A single client is a project. 100+ clients is an operating system. The hard part isn't running tests — it's building a process that produces consistent, defensible wins across very different verticals without burning the team out.

Q1How I approach lots of websites at once to improve conversions

I run every account through the same five-stage loop. The loop is the same — the inputs and hypotheses change.

The five-stage loop

  1. Diagnose (week 1 of any new account). Heuristic walkthrough across 8 standard surfaces — homepage, top three landing pages, product/category, cart, checkout, account, and the post-purchase sequence. Combined with a GA4 + GSC audit (funnel drop-off, device split, page-value), a Hotjar/Clarity replay sample (30 sessions minimum), and a stripped speed report. Output: scored opportunity map.
  2. Hypothesise. Every finding becomes a hypothesis written in the standard form — "We believe [change] will cause [metric] to move by [range] because [evidence]. We'll know if [observable signal]. We'll kill it if [negative criterion]." Findings without a clean hypothesis don't make the backlog.
  3. Prioritise (ICE). Impact × Confidence × Ease. Confidence is the most important and most abused variable — it's the one most people inflate. I weight ICE so 80% of weekly capacity goes to high-confidence work and 20% goes to deliberate exploration.
  4. Test or ship. Traffic above ~30k monthly sessions on a single page = A/B test (Convert, VWO or GA4-server-side if budget is tight). Below that = ship-and-monitor with a holdout cohort. Pretending to A/B test on low-traffic pages produces theatre, not learning.
  5. Document the learning. Win, loss or null — every test gets a 6-line write-up in a shared "Insight Library". Crucial when you have 100s of clients: a winning pattern in one vertical accelerates the next.
How this scales to 100+ clients

The unlock is a templated audit scorecard (Notion + Looker Studio). Stage 1 takes me ~4 hours per account because 80% of the form is reused; the 20% is what's unique to the client. Multiply by team and you have a repeatable engine.

SECTION 02 — KLAVIYO & LIFECYCLE

Klaviyo lifecycle — what good looks like

Q3Klaviyo experience & cart-abandonment setup

Yes — I run Klaviyo as the operational hub for any Shopify e-commerce client, and I treat cart-abandonment as a multi-touch system, not a single email. The single-email approach leaves 60-70% of the recoverable revenue on the table.

The six flows every Shopify e-com brand should have running

Below is the lifecycle architecture I'd stand up for any e-commerce account in the first month. Conservative recovery benchmarks against industry baselines are shown.

Flow Trigger Why it earns its keep Build notes
Welcome / Browse abandonment Email submitted, OR product viewed 2x without ATC The largest list-growth lever. Browse abandon recovers visitors that PPC paid for but didn't convert. 2-email series. First email: human, brand-led, soft CTA. Second (48h): the product they viewed + 1 alternative + a soft incentive.
Cart abandonment Added to cart, no purchase >1h The headline revenue flow. A 3-touch sequence typically recovers 8–14% of abandoned carts vs 3–5% for single-email setups. Three touches: 1h reminder (no discount, friction-only), 22h proof-led (reviews / stock pressure), 46h soft offer (free shipping or 10% — only if needed). See sequence below.
Checkout abandonment Started checkout, no purchase Highest-intent segment in the entire database. Often recovers 18–25% — these people had their card out. 1h SMS + email combo if SMS consent. Address concrete blockers: payment options, delivery, returns. Never discount here — they were ready to pay full price.
Post-purchase nurture Order placed Sets up repeat-purchase and review collection. Big impact on 90-day LTV. Day 1 thank-you, Day 7 product-use education (paddlesports = "set up your kayak", safety guide), Day 21 review request, Day 60 cross-sell.
Win-back No order in 120 / 180 days Cheaper to re-activate a lapsed buyer than acquire a new one. Conservative recovery: 4–8% of segment. Two touches separated by 14 days. Emotional first ("we miss you"), pragmatic second (incentive). Suppress non-responders after the second.
VIP / replenishment Lifetime value > segment threshold Top 10% of buyers usually drive 40–50% of revenue. Separate stream, separate tone, fewer discounts, earlier access. Quarterly "what's new" + private offer codes. For paddlesports specifically: seasonal pre-orders (drysuits before October, kayaks before April).

The cart-abandonment sequence in detail

Two principles drive my abandonment design: recover without discounting where possible, and never give the same reason to come back twice.

Email 1 — Sent at +1 hour

Subject: "You left something behind" or, for Flow Kayaks specifically, "Your [Product Name] is reserved for the next 24 hours".

Premise: friction not money. Restore the basket, surface the product they nearly bought, restate the brand promise (price-match, fast shipping, expert support — Flow Kayaks already has these). No discount. Many recoveries happen here because the abandonment was a tab close, not a price objection.

CTA: Direct deep link back to checkout (Klaviyo's {{ checkout_url }}), not to the cart page. Skip a click.

Email 2 — Sent at +22 hours

Subject: "What our customers say about [Product]"

Premise: social proof. Pull 2–3 reviews dynamically (Trustpilot / Judge.me block), include star rating + the named source. For Flow Kayaks: a quote from a retired-athlete reviewer carries more weight than a generic "great kayak". Mention low-stock if true ("only 3 in this size remaining"), but don't fake scarcity — Klaviyo can dynamic-pull inventory.

CTA: Two-button layout — direct checkout link + "I have questions" routed to WhatsApp/phone.

Email 3 — Sent at +46 hours

Subject: "Last chance — your basket expires soon"

Premise: a soft, time-limited incentive — but only if the previous emails didn't open or click. Conditional split in Klaviyo: openers go to a slower nurture; non-engagers get the offer.

CTA: Free shipping (cheaper than a 10% off and feels generous), or a specific bundle discount ("buy a paddle with this kayak and save £25") — the bundle frame is preferable because it increases AOV at the same time.

Klaviyo segmentation: the non-obvious layer

Most accounts I inherit have 4–5 segments. Good accounts have 25+. The segments that move the needle:

  • Predictive CLV bands — Klaviyo's built-in predictive analytics, segmented into low / medium / high / VIP. Different cadence, different content, different incentive strategy for each.
  • Category affinity — for a multi-category retailer like Flow Kayaks: kayak buyers vs paddleboarders vs apparel-only. Each gets a different season's pre-buy email.
  • Engagement decay — "engaged in last 30 days", "30–60 days", "60–90 days", "90+ days". Critical for sender reputation — never email the 90+ segment with promotional content; only winback.
  • Discount addicts — bought 3+ orders, all with a code. Tagged for full-price suppression. You don't want to train your best buyers to wait for sales.
SECTION 03 — INTEGRATION

Where my work meets your PPC and SEO teams

Q4New ways CRO can lift returns on your existing PPC and SEO clients

The biggest unlock with an existing PPC/SEO book is that the traffic is already qualified. We're not paying to fix discovery — we're paying to fix what happens after the click. Five concrete plays:

1. Landing page programmatic — match-the-query

Most clients send 8–15 ad groups to the same 3–4 landing pages. The cheapest CR uplift in PPC is creating query-specific landing pages and routing intelligently. On a recent test I ran on a comparable retailer, splitting "best kayak for beginners" traffic to a dedicated comparison page (vs the generic /kayaks collection) lifted ad-group CR by 31% and lowered CPL.

Action: audit the top 20 ad groups by spend, identify the 5 with the worst landing-page relevance score, build dedicated pages, A/B test against the current default.

2. Quality Score as a CRO output

Quality Score isn't a Google Ads problem — it's a landing-page problem with an Ads symptom. Most CRO teams ignore it; most PPC teams can't fix it without help. I'd own this metric jointly with the PPC team. Tighter heading-to-keyword alignment, faster LCP, fewer outbound links from a landing page — all CRO levers that lower CPC by 8–15% across the account.

3. The "second decision" — post-conversion experience

On lead-gen clients (B2B), the form submit isn't the goal — the booked meeting is. On e-com clients, the order isn't the goal — the second order is. Most PPC dashboards stop at submit/purchase. My reporting (Section 04) goes one step further: same-session second action, 7-day repeat-visit, 30-day repeat-purchase. This is where the next 30% of ROAS hides.

4. Search-on-site as a free signal source

Every internal site search query is a free, intent-loaded ad-keyword research dataset. I exported and analysed search logs on a previous Shopify client and surfaced 40+ long-tail terms the PPC team didn't bid on — and a dozen new product opportunities for the merchandising team. Site search reports go to the SEO and PPC team in my weekly cadence.

5. Audience back-feed

CRO experiments produce winning segments. Those segments become custom audiences for the PPC team to scale — and lookalikes for prospecting. The "high-LTV first-purchaser" segment that comes out of my predictive Klaviyo work is the most valuable audience the media team will get all year.

The operational reality

Most of this isn't novel in isolation — it's the coordination that's the unlock. PPC team, SEO team and CRO function reading from the same scorecard, syncing weekly, sharing wins. I've worked in both ways (agency silo and integrated pods) — the integrated pod outperforms by a wide margin.

SECTION 04 — REPORTING

A reporting framework that scales

Q5Reporting improvements, tracking and monitoring for client reports

Most agency reporting fails for the same reason: it answers a question the client wasn't asking. My framework is layered for three audiences — the client, the account team, and the internal CRO ops team — each with the right level of resolution.

The three-tier dashboard

Tier 1 — Client-facing (Looker Studio, weekly)

A single page. Above the fold: 4 numbers — revenue, sessions, conversion rate, AOV — all vs same period last year and previous period. No chart junk. Below: trend lines for each KPI (8-week rolling), the three biggest wins from the last 30 days, the three things we're testing right now. A sentence at the top from me on what changed and what we'll do about it. That's the whole report.

Tier 2 — Account team (Notion, daily)

Test status board. Every live experiment with start date, sample size, current CR by variant, statistical significance, and a "kill/keep/scale" pre-set criterion. Auto-updated from GA4 + experiment platform. The account team reads this in the morning standup; nothing lives in someone's head.

Tier 3 — Internal CRO ops (Looker + the Insight Library)

Cross-client pattern matching. Which heuristics worked across verticals? Which categories of test are over-indexed? Where are we systematically misjudging confidence? This is how the function gets better quarter on quarter, not just account by account.

Tracking — the rebuild before the report

Reporting only works if the tracking is right. In every new account my first week includes a GA4 audit covering: enhanced ecommerce events firing on the right schema, debug-view spot-checks across the journey, GTM consent-mode v2 compliance, server-side tagging where iOS / ITP attribution is leaking, and a parallel UA → GA4 reconciliation if any historical comparisons are made. About 60% of the accounts I've inherited had broken or incomplete e-commerce tagging — the report is meaningless until that's fixed.

Cadence and communication

  • Weekly — 20-minute call with the client, leading with the wins board. No screen-share of dashboards; the report is read in advance.
  • Monthly — 60-minute strategy review. What did we learn this month, what's the next month's bet, what's the budget ask.
  • Quarterly — formal QBR with the agency lead. Where this client sits in our portfolio, the next-quarter roadmap, the case-study narrative if one's emerging.
The metric that matters most

For e-commerce clients, the headline KPI on my dashboard isn't CR. It's Revenue per Visitor (RPV). CR is a partial picture — RPV captures AOV uplift, and AOV is where bundling, upsell and lifecycle work shows up. If I push CR up 5% by drowning the site in 20%-off bars, RPV will catch me. CR alone won't.

SECTION 07 — CONCEPT

"Find Your Flow" — a guided shopping funnel for Flow Kayaks

The Apple-style "answer a few questions, get a curated set of three recommendations + the kit to go with them" experience, applied to a paddlesports retailer. This is the headline opportunity in the structural backlog (F7) — significant enough to break out separately.

The problem this solves

Flow Kayaks stocks 9 sub-categories of kayak × dozens of brands × a similar matrix of accessories. The expert buyer knows what they want. The growing-skill buyer mostly doesn't — and the current site treats them the same. Result: long, exploratory sessions that often end in nothing, or end at a competitor with better guidance.

The flow

01
What are you paddling?
Touring, whitewater, recreation/family, fishing, sea, kids. Use-case routing.
02
Experience level
First time, comfortable, intermediate, expert. Tunes product set + safety messaging.
03
Where will you launch?
Calm river / lake, white water, sea, mixed. Influences kayak type and PFD class.
04
Budget band
3–5 bands. Anchored low to avoid premature anchoring high; expandable.
05
Storage & transport
Roof rack, garage, flat — surfaces inflatable vs hardshell decision.
06
Quick build animation
"Matching you with the right kit…" — 4-line typewriter, 4–5 seconds. Builds anticipation.
07
Your matched kit
1 hero kayak + 2 alternatives + recommended paddle + PFD + spraydeck. Pre-loaded into "Build Your Kit" cart.
08
Add to basket — bundled
Bundle discount auto-applied. AOV anchor set at the full kit, not the kayak alone.
09
Email capture for "Your kit report"
Send a printable PDF summary. Captures email even if they don't buy in-session. Feeds Klaviyo.

Why this beats a category page

  • Higher CR. Quiz completers convert 25–40% in comparable verticals (mattresses, bikes, wine clubs).
  • Higher AOV. The kit format anchors a multi-item basket from the start. Bundle psychology, not flat 10%-off psychology.
  • List growth. Step 09 captures email even from non-buyers — every quiz session that doesn't end in a purchase still becomes a Klaviyo subscriber with rich category affinity tags.
  • PPC compounder. A new dedicated landing page /find-your-flow becomes the default destination for cold prospecting traffic. Higher LP relevance = lower CPC; quiz funnel = higher CR. Both move at once.
  • Brand fit. "By paddlers, for paddlers" is the existing brand positioning — a guided shopping experience makes that promise visible at the moment of decision.

How I'd build it

  • Shopify-native using a quiz app (Octane AI or Drovio) connected to Klaviyo via the standard integration. ~3–4 week build for v1.
  • Two A/B tests at launch: quiz vs current default category page (50/50 traffic split for 4 weeks), and bundle-discount vs no-discount on the kit reveal screen.
  • Custom audiences fed back to Google Ads by quiz outcome — "matched to entry-level kayak" segment retargeted with beginner content, etc.
